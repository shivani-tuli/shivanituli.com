Build 01

Blood-Brain Barrier-on-Chip

A multi-cellular microfluidic platform that models cancer metastasis across the blood-brain barrier in real-time.

The Problem

Brain metastasis is the leading cause of death in patients with advanced cancers — yet we have no reliable way to model how cancer cells breach the blood-brain barrier outside of animal experiments. Traditional transwell assays are static, lack physiological shear stress, and can't capture the multi-cellular crosstalk that governs extravasation.

What I Built

I developed a multi-cellular BBB-on-chip model incorporating endothelial cells, pericytes, astrocytes, and neurons — the four critical cell types that form the neurovascular unit. The chip features serpentine microfluidic channels with controlled shear stress mimicking physiological blood flow conditions.

The platform enables real-time visualization of cancer cell extravasation across the blood-brain barrier under a confocal microscope. Fluorescently labeled tumor cells are introduced into the vascular channel, and I image their transmigration through the endothelial barrier in real-time — something no static transwell can do.

🎯
This platform enables therapeutic screening — testing whether new drugs can prevent cancer cells from crossing the BBB, all in a device smaller than a credit card. Presented at the ASCB Conference 2024.

Build 02

Tissue Engineered Blood Vessels

Bioprinted implantable blood vessels with computational models for bidirectional flow dynamics.

The Build

At Harvard University's Zhang Lab, I developed implantable tissue-engineered blood vessels using two-phase bioprinting materials. The goal: create synthetic vascular grafts that can replace damaged arteries and integrate seamlessly with living tissue.

I didn't just print vessels — I built the computational backbone. I created CFD models simulating bidirectional flow dynamics through micropatterned surfaces, optimizing channel geometries to promote endothelial cell alignment and reduce thrombogenicity. Then I wrote automated image processing algorithms to characterize cellular responses in vessel-on-chip platforms — turning hours of manual microscopy analysis into minutes.

My CFD paper on micropatterned surface optimization was published in Bioengineering (2024, 11, 1092) and nominated for the Editor's Choice Award.

Build 03

PalpAid: Breast Cancer Diagnostic

A quantitative, low-cost handheld breast palpation device designed for at-home use and primary care settings.

The Problem

Breast cancer screening shouldn't require a $250,000 machine — or a hospital visit. For the billions of women without access to mammography, a lump found too late is a death sentence.

Clinical breast examination is subjective — two clinicians feeling the same lump will disagree on size, hardness, and location. Self-examination has even lower sensitivity. There's no quantitative, affordable tool that a woman can use at home to track breast tissue changes over time.

What I Built

At Cornell University's Antaki Lab, I engineered PalpAid — a handheld palpation device prototype incorporating optimized optical components and shape-from-shading algorithms for quantitative breast tumor detection. The device interfaces directly with a smartphone app featuring palpation visualization, lump location mapping, and lesion tracking.

I didn't just build the engineering prototype — I validated the need. I led SBIR Phase-I and BRCP grant proposals and conducted 40+ stakeholder interviews through the NSF I-Corps program, speaking with clinicians, patients, and insurance executives to refine the product-market fit.

To test the device, I developed mechanical breast tissue analogs using poly(vinyl alcohol) with chaotropic salt inclusions — phantom tissues that mimic the mechanical properties of real breast tissue with embedded "tumors" of varying hardness and depth.

Build 04

BioDot: Handheld CBC Device

A point-of-care complete blood count device using microfluidics and dielectrophoretic separation — for underserved healthcare settings.

What I Built

I developed a point-of-care CBC device using microfluidics and dielectrophoretic separation to replicate large-scale CBC machinery in a handheld form factor. A standard CBC machine costs $50,000+, fills a room, and needs a trained technician. BioDot fits in your hand.

The device measures 160mm × 100mm × 50mm — smaller than a smartphone in two dimensions. A finger-prick blood sample flows through microfluidic channels where dielectrophoretic forces separate white blood cells, red blood cells, and platelets based on their electrical properties. Optical sensors count each population.

Results in 2-4 minutes with 90-100% accuracy. Designed for community health workers in underserved settings — no lab, no technician, no refrigeration.

Build 05

Software Tools Suite

Computational tools for biological image analysis — from ImageJ macros converted to standalone apps, to browser-based analyzers deployed on GitHub Pages.

The Philosophy

Lab tools shouldn't require a $1,000 software license or a 20-step ImageJ macro. I build free, open-source, browser-based analysis tools that run entirely in the client — no server, no installation, no login. Open a URL, drop your image, get results.

When a browser can't do the job, I convert legacy ImageJ plugins into standalone Python applications with Tkinter GUIs and PyInstaller packaging — cross-platform, one-click executables that any researcher can download and run.

Build 07

Drug Discovery & Scaffold Engineering

From novel cancer drug candidates to bone regeneration scaffolds — the foundational wet-lab research that shaped my engineering instincts.

DDX3 Inhibitor — Johns Hopkins University

At Johns Hopkins University (2019), I investigated the therapeutic efficacy of RK-33, a novel DDX3 pathway inhibitor, for breast and colon cancer treatment. DDX3 is overexpressed in aggressive cancers and drives tumor progression through Wnt signaling — blocking it with RK-33 showed promising cytotoxicity in multiple cancer cell lines.

I performed cell viability assays, protein expression profiling via Bradford Assay, Western blots, and single-cell RNA sequencing analysis to characterize the transcriptomic response of cancer cells to RK-33 treatment.

DDX3 signaling pathway and RK-33 inhibition mechanism
Bone Regeneration Scaffolds — Pitt Pharmaceutical Sciences

At the University of Pittsburgh School of Pharmaceutical Sciences (2017–2018), I built polysaccharide hydrogel beaded scaffolds for bone regeneration. The non-toxic scaffolds were implanted in mice skull models for drug delivery testing.

I performed decalcification and storage of mice skulls, created wax blocks using a HistoEmbedder, and sectioned tissue for histological analysis. This was my first real research experience — the project that showed me what it means to build something with your hands and see it work inside a living system.
